# Explaining with pictures, for grown-ups at work

The people watching are colleagues, not pupils. They want the point, the reason, and what it changes for
them. Show what you are talking about while you say it, put the important word where the eye already is,
and stop when it has landed. No "today we will learn", no recap of the recap.

Everything here was seen in real videos and measured (Loom: `docs/research/2026-10-01-visual-study.md`,
`docs/research/2026-10-01-channels-compared.md`, the per-channel notes under `docs/research/`, and the 96-film
TED-Ed study, `docs/research/vision/2026-10-05-tedEd-full-conclusions.md`). It is a
set of moves to choose from, not a checklist. The person making the video picks the register; any move can
be borrowed into any register, except those marked illustrated packs only.

## 1. Turn the concept into a picture (pick the move that fits the idea)
1. **Personify the abstraction, then reveal what it really is.** A risky loan drawn as something harmless,
   then shown as it is, with its three properties written beside it as they are said.
2. **Label the claim on the object.** The pile gets a sign that says what people pretended it was. The gap
   between the label and the thing IS the lesson.
3. **Show the transaction: who gives what to whom.** Money moving from one desk to another explains a
   conflict of interest faster than a sentence about incentives. Follow it with an everyday analogy, drawn
   literally (a critic paid only for good reviews).
4. **Put the number on the furniture, or make it the quantity.** On a price tag, a contract, a ledger line, a
   notice on the wall, a flip calendar; or a unit grid stamped to the share, a counter locking on a pile
   (`Loom.data`). A number floating beside a scene reads as a slide; a number in the scene reads as a fact.
5. **The everyday thing, then the formal picture.** A switch, then doors that can be open, closed or in
   between, and only then the model. The analogy goes first so the model has somewhere to land.
6. **One figure, one problem at a time, one overlay each.** Keep the same character (or object) and add one
   visual layer per point (a dashed outline for pressure, a cut-away for the inside, a tag for a temperature,
   a band for a risk). Go closer when the problem is inside.
7. **Physical model, then diagram, then the diagram asks the question.** Boards on a table become a 2x2 that
   fills cell by cell; the mechanism moves inside its cell; the last cell is left as the open question.
8. **Cut from the story to a plain pictogram for the mechanism, then back.** In a story video, the HOW is often
   clearest as two minimal pictures on a white ground (machines on a line versus people at a bench), then
   the story resumes.
9. **Hold the layout, change its state** (the default for mechanism and structure). Draw the system once;
   add, swap, recolour or relabel on the spoken word (`Loom.hold`), so nobody loses their place (one TED-Ed
   diagram held 44 s). In a story look the board can be a place: one castle rebuilt from the same angle, each
   new part painted as it is named. *D-112: this said "nothing is replaced"; the films swap in place as often
   as they add.*
10. **Same pose, two contexts.** The old, obvious version, then the same figure or object, same pose, in the
    viewer's world, threat swapped: a predator, then an email lettered like a horror title. One picture
    says "your body cannot tell these apart".
11. **A list is objects.** One slot whose contents swap per keyword, items arriving on their words (about
    1.5 s per spoken item), or small scenes with the number on the prop (an inbox reading 6,616), remembered
    as places. End on the key item, clear, then show the exception alone. Never bullets or an icon grid.
    The swap slot is coming; until then, items arrive in one fixed place.
12. **Open it on the naming word.** Show the closed whole, then lift or peel it so the inside plays in place
    (`Loom.hold.lift`, `peel`). A magnifier inset on a leader line is coming; until then, zoom into a child
    station.
13. **Expected, then actual.** Hold the expected version until believed, then swap the actual into the same
    place, labels unmoved, or strike it in the accent and keep it visible (a morph is coming).
14. **Annotate in place, not a cut to a chart.** Lay the value or line over the scene that enacts it
    (`Loom.data.barsOn`, `dipLine`, `tag`); a chart, if any, is a short summary after (rare in the films).
15. **Recap by replay:** the lesson's own images return (`intent: "callback"`), never a recap slide.

### Illustrated packs only (look_family: illustrated: story-cartoon, flat-cosmos, glow-flat, whiteboard, cinematic-noir, cinematic-dawn)
- **Personified agents:** one small character with a fixed silhouette, colour and prop does every step.
- **Hands acting on objects:** a flat hand enters on the action word, does one thing, leaves (39 films). The
  hands kit is coming; until then the object changes by itself.
- **Small posed people** in a composed place: minimal faces, profile or back views, emotion in posture;
  designed rigs only, never drawn by code from imagination (D-102).
Editorial and technical packs carry these ideas with objects and labels.

## 2. Emphasise on purpose (and know why it works)
| move | use it for | why |
|---|---|---|
| The word on screen as it is said (on a prop, a pill, a card) | the one term to keep | eye and ear get the same thing at the same moment |
| One saturated thing; everything else grey or dimmed (`accent_on`) | what is being discussed right now | attention goes to the single difference; placement, not size |
| A black card with one short line (`intent: "punctuation"`, 0.6 to 1.2 s) | a turn: but, until, and still | a rest for the eye, a pivot for the ear |
| A full-frame lettered card for a new term, held a few seconds | a word the viewer must keep (a product, a rule, a role) | the term gets its own moment before it is used |
| A pill label on the thing, colour by category, on its word, cleared before the next | naming parts while they move | the name stays attached to what it names; never a legend |
| A closer shot of the same figure | what matters more, or what is inside | scale says importance without words |
| A contradiction in one image (gold and a warning sign) | a hidden risk | the clash is the message |
| The real source with one phrase highlighted | a claim the viewer may doubt | credibility shown, not asserted |
| The opening image coming back, changed (`intent: "callback"`) | the payoff | recall of the start, now understood |
One loud thing at a time. When the next cell lights, the last one calms down. Texture signage may be
`intent: "diegetic_text"`; a narrated term never is.

**A finished frame may rest** while the voice explains (7 to 24 s in the films; `intent: "rest"` or
`"process_hold"`, up to `pace.rest_max_s`). A long hold on an empty frame is still the failure.

## 3. How full the frame was in the references (measured, by register)
Share of the frame with something drawn in it (16 px blocks with an edge):
- **Story cartoon: about 0.40 to 0.50.** Every shot is a place (a room, a street, a stage) with props. The
  information lives on the props.
- **Presenter documentary: about 0.30 to 0.46.** The diagram IS the frame, or the footage is. Covered area is
  very high (0.80 to 0.88): no empty page.
- **Board (math, systems): about 0.11 to 0.16.** Black on purpose, thin strokes, one board built up.
- **Textured story (TED-Ed): about 0.20 to 0.49,** with covered 0.71 to 0.91. A paper, parchment or crayon
  ground fills the frame even where little is drawn.
- **Saturated flat (newer Kurzgesagt): about 0.28 to 0.33,** yet it reads as full. Covered is 0.62 to 0.84 and
  vivid colour is the highest measured; colour fields and glow fill the frame, not line work.

So a frame can be full through props and outline, through colour and light, or through texture.

Loom lessons measured 0.13 to 0.28, with whole frames that were one colour; small cards on a big page tend
to read as slides. These are measurements of a few channels, not targets: use them to notice what a frame is
doing (a place, a diagram that is the frame, or a deliberate board) and to choose on purpose. Measure with
`tools/style_fingerprint.py` when a look is in doubt.

## 4. The voice: a capable colleague, not a teacher
- **Say the point first, then show why.** "Refunds over 500 need a second check. Here is what happened when one
  didn't get it."
- **Use the viewer's world.** Their tools, their meetings, their numbers, not a classroom example.
- **Short sentences, active verbs, concrete nouns.** "The review sat for two days" beats "there was a delay in
  the review process".
- **No throat-clearing:** no "in this video", no "let's dive in", no "as we learned earlier".
- **Ask the real question,** the one they would ask: "So who checks the checker?" Then answer it on screen.
- **Respect their time.** If the picture already said it, don't say it again. End when it has landed.
- **Grounding still rules.** Every number and claim traces to the source. Dramatised moments and characters
  are framed as illustrations ("say a request comes in on Friday").
