# Product explainers: introduce it, sell it honestly, make it make sense

A product video has three jobs, in a mix the format decides:

- **Introduce:** what this is, who it is for, in the viewer's words, within seconds.
- **Sell:** why it beats what the viewer does today, proven on screen, ending on one ask.
- **Make it make sense:** the mental model of how it works, so the viewer can picture using it.

It is not a lesson and not a concept explainer:

- **Teaching:** the viewer leaves able to DO a task, and is checked. A product video does not test anyone.
- **Explainer:** the viewer leaves understanding a mechanism. A product video is about a choice, so the mechanism
  matters only as far as it makes the benefit believable.
- **Onboarding or how-to:** after the purchase, the job is getting work done. That is teaching. Hand it to a
  teaching skill.

The viewer is the hero. The product is the tool the hero picks up. Every beat answers the question in the viewer's
head: "what does this do for me, and do I believe it?"

## 1. Before any script: the product truth sheet

Fill this in from the product's own material: site, docs, release notes, the product itself, approved case studies
and the product owner. Every line names its source. A blank line is a question for the owner, never a guess.

| Field | What it holds | Test |
|---|---|---|
| **Product, one line** | what it is, in a category the viewer already knows ("a booking tool for", "an API that") | A stranger could repeat it. |
| **The one viewer** | role, situation and what they want (e.g. a finance lead closing the month) | One person, not a segment list. |
| **Their status quo** | what they do today: the real competitive alternative, often a spreadsheet, email, a person or doing nothing | Named concretely. |
| **The cost of the status quo** | time, errors, risk, money or stress, in their terms | Sourced or framed as typical, never invented. |
| **Unique attributes** | what this does that the status quo cannot (2 or 3) | Each is true today, in the shipped product. |
| **Value** | the outcome each attribute gives ("so that ...") | Phrased as a result, not a feature. |
| **The aha moment** | the one screen or action where the value becomes obvious | It can be shown in under 10 seconds. |
| **Proof inventory** | numbers, customer quotes, logos, certifications, awards, each with its source, date, conditions and permission | Nothing on screen that is not on this list. |
| **The one ask** | the single next step (try free, book a demo, open the feature, read the docs) | One verb, one place. |
| **Constraints** | brand assets and rules, what must not be shown (roadmap, customer data, internal tools), legal review owner, markets | Written before storyboarding. |

Positioning comes first, so the video does not invent it. The fields above follow April Dunford's components:
competitive alternatives, unique attributes, value, target customers and market category. If the owner cannot
answer them, the video is not ready. Say so, because a video cannot fix unclear positioning.

## 2. Pick the format by where it plays and what the viewer already knows

The lengths are industry conventions from vendor and agency guidance. They are directional, not measured laws.

| Format | Where | Length | Job mix | Ask |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Teaser or ad** | paid social, launch posts | 6, 15 or 30 s | introduce 60, sell 40 | soft (learn more) |
| **Homepage or hero explainer** | the landing page | 60 to 90 s | introduce 30, sell 40, understand 30 | try or book |
| **Feature launch** | release post, in-app, email | 30 to 60 s | what changed, why now, where it lives | open the feature |
| **Product demo or sales follow-up** | after interest, sent by sales | 2 to 3 min | sell 30, understand 50, proof 20 | book a call or start a trial |
| **Technical how-it-works** | developer or IT evaluators | 2 to 4 min | understand 70: architecture, guarantees, integration | docs, sandbox |
| **Internal rollout** | employees getting a new tool | 60 to 120 s | why we are changing, what is better for you, when, where to get help | where to start |

- **Awareness:** a viewer who has never heard of it needs the problem first.
- **Decision stage:** a viewer comparing options needs the product on screen early and proof.
- **Exec viewer:** outcomes and risk.
- **User viewer:** the workflow.
- **Technical evaluator:** how it fits, what it guarantees, what it costs to adopt.

## 3. The arcs

The default arc runs as follows. Starred roles are required.

1. **Hook\*:** the viewer's moment, in the first 3 seconds, readable with the sound off.
   - A recognisable situation ("Month end. Forty tabs open.").
   - Or a result they want ("Every invoice matched by 9 am.").
   - Never a logo sting, a company history or "Introducing".
2. **Status quo\*:** what they do today and what it costs them. Name the alternative fairly, and keep it short.
   Agitation is one beat, not a horror film.
3. **The shift\*:** one sentence on what changes ("Now the matching happens as invoices arrive."). The product name
   lands here, with its one-line description.
4. **Show it working\*:** two or three value moments. Each one runs benefit (said), then the feature doing it (shown),
   then the result on screen (held). Lead with the aha moment.
5. **How it works:** the mental model, only as deep as this viewer needs to trust it. For example: connect, rules,
   review; or a diagram of the data flow.
6. **Proof\*:** the strongest single item from the proof inventory, attributed. One great proof beats a logo wall.
7. **The ask\*:** one call to action, said and shown, then the end card (product name, the ask, the address) held
   for at least 3 seconds.

**Other shapes** (pick per product; the default stays the default):
- **Day in the life, before and after:** the same person and the same task, the old way and then the new way, with
  the time saved shown, not stated.
- **Aha first (cold open):** open on the result screen, then rewind ("Here is how it got there.").
- **Feature launch:** what is new (shown at once), why it matters, where to find it, what it replaces. No status
  quo act.
- **Objection ladder** (sales follow-up): the three doubts a buyer has, each answered with the product on screen.
- **Technical evaluator:** the architecture in one picture, the request's path through it, guarantees (security,
  limits, uptime, only as sourced), integration in three steps, then docs.
- **Internal rollout:** why we are changing (the honest reason), what gets easier for you, what stays the same, the
  date, where help is. Never sell internally with hype. People see through it, and they have no choice anyway.

**Timing guide for 90 seconds:** about 220 to 240 spoken words at a calm pace.
- hook 0 to 5 s;
- status quo 5 to 15 s;
- shift 15 to 22 s;
- value moments 22 to 65 s;
- proof 65 to 75 s;
- ask and end card 75 to 90 s.

## 4. Messaging: benefit, then feature, then proof

- **The "so that" ladder.** Write each feature and climb with "so that" until a viewer would care. Say the top rung
  and show the bottom rung.
  - Example: "auto-matching", so that "you do not tick boxes", so that "the month closes in a day". You say "close
    the month in a day" and you show auto-matching.
- **One idea per beat.** If a beat needs "and", it is two beats or one too many.
- **The viewer's words, not the roadmap's.** No internal feature names, no acronyms the buyer does not use, no
  "leverage, seamless, robust, powerful, revolutionary, next-generation, unlock, supercharge, game-changing,
  best-in-class, AI-powered" as a claim by itself.
- **Specific over superlative.** "Matches 3 currencies" beats "handles everything". A specific claim needs a source.
  A superlative needs one too, and is usually not worth having.
- **Name it once, early, then show it.** The product name at the shift, on screen with the logo. After that, "it" and
  the screen do the work.
- **Features you leave out are a decision.** A homepage video shows 2 or 3 value moments, not the feature list. A
  feature tour is the commonest failure of the genre.
- **The viewer can answer three questions at the end:** what it is, why it is better for them, and what to do next.
  If any answer is fuzzy, cut until it is not.

## 5. Showing software: the craft that decides whether it lands

**Choose the fidelity per beat** (one video may use several):

| Fidelity | When | Rules |
|---|---|---|
| **Real capture** | decision-stage viewers, demos, anything the viewer will do themselves | Current build only; real flows; cleaned data. |
| **Cleaned capture** | most product beats | Hide clutter, notifications, unrelated panels, personal bookmarks; fixed window size; one zoom level. |
| **Simplified UI (SUI)** | awareness, concept beats, fast montages, small screens | Blocks of colour standing for the real layout, the one element that matters drawn in full. It must stay faithful: never show a feature, button or result the product does not have. Pair it with a heading or narration that names what it is. |
| **Abstract or diagram** | the invisible parts: sync, AI, APIs, security, integrations | Show the flow, not the code. Isometric for systems that live in places, light for AI or security, a drawn flow for a process. |

**Focus: one region at a time.**
- Push in on the region that matters, dim or blur the rest, and pull back out to re-orient.
- Each zoom eases in and out, and it never happens mid-click.
- Hold the result: the screen after the action is the payoff, and it stays still long enough to read, about
  1.5 to 2 s plus 0.3 s per word on screen.

**The pointer is an actor.**
- It moves in smooth, purposeful paths, and every click is visible (a pulse, a press).
- It never wiggles to point at something; use a highlight instead.
- One action, then the screen answers. The action and its result are seen in the same frame, so the viewer sees
  cause and effect.

**Time.**
- Typing is sped up, loading is cut, and long waits get a time-lapse tag ("3 hours later").
- Never fake speed the product does not have. If it takes 40 seconds, say "under a minute" or show the tag.

**Sample data** (the most visible detail and the most often wrong):
- It is invented, plausible and consistent across every beat: the same customer, amounts and dates throughout.
- No real customer or personal data, and no lorem ipsum.
- Names are diverse and ordinary, and numbers add up on screen.
- The dates are recent but not dated to a specific day, so the video ages slowly.

**Context.** Show where the product lives (a device frame, a person at work, the phone in a hand) once early, then
go full screen.

**Brand.** Use the product's own brand assets, from the owner, under their brand rules. Never another company's
logo, interface or colours, unless it is an integration the owner has the right to show, and then as that company's
guidelines allow.

## 6. Selling honestly: claims, proof and disclosures

This section states general principles from US FTC guidance and common practice. It is not legal advice. Every
product video that makes claims needs review by the owner's legal or compliance reviewer, in each market it runs in.

- **Every objective claim needs substantiation in hand before it ships**, express or implied: speed, savings, accuracy,
  uptime, "fastest", "most secure", "number 1". The truth sheet names the source, the conditions and the date.
  - "Up to" claims need the typical result to be close to the maximum, or the typical result shown too.
  - A number on screen carries its basis in small print or in narration ("in our 2026 customer survey, n=").
- **Comparisons with competitors:**
  - Truthful comparisons on measurable attributes are allowed, and they need the same substantiation as any claim.
  - Naming a rival raises the stakes. Use the alternative category ("spreadsheets", "manual review") unless the
    owner's legal reviewer approves the named comparison and the test behind it.
- **Testimonials and reviews:**
  - Use real customers, with their permission, saying what they actually said. Trim a quote; do not change it.
  - A testimonial must not imply results most customers will not get. If it does, disclose what is typical, clearly.
  - Disclose any connection (paid, employee, free product) that the viewer would weigh.
  - Actors or composites are labelled as such.
- **Logos and awards:** only customers who agreed to be shown, and only awards and certifications currently held, with
  their year.
- **Security and compliance badges** (SOC 2, ISO 27001, HIPAA, GDPR): only what is certified now. Say "compliant"
  only with the reviewer's approval.
- **AI claims:** say what the AI does in plain words and where a human stays in the loop. Never imply accuracy or
  autonomy the product does not have. Show a real output, not an idealised one.
- **Pricing and trials:** terms that change the offer go on screen near the offer, readable: "free for 14 days, card
  required". Prices carry their currency and period.
- **Roadmap:** never show an unshipped feature as shipped. "Coming soon" is labelled on screen, and the reviewer
  approves it.
- **Disclosures in video:** said and shown when the claim is said and shown. They sit next to the claim, are
  readable at the viewing size, and are on screen long enough to read. In short vertical video, centre them in
  contrast. A corner line at 12 px is not a disclosure.
- **Accessibility is part of honesty:** accurate captions, readable contrast and no information carried by colour
  alone.

## 7. Sound off, captions, versions

- **Most feeds start muted.** The first 3 seconds must work with no sound: the hook as on-screen words or a picture
  that explains itself. Test it by watching the opening muted.
- **Captions on every version,** burned in for social, as a file for the site. On-screen copy carries the arc for
  muted viewers. Captions that duplicate on-screen words get trimmed, not doubled.
- **Cut down from the master, plan it in the storyboard:**
  - mark the beats that survive a 30 s cut (hook, shift, aha, ask) and a 15 s cut (hook, aha, ask);
  - a 6 s bumper is one picture and one line.
- **Aspect ratios:**
  - 16:9 for site and YouTube;
  - 1:1 and 4:5 for feeds;
  - 9:16 for stories and short video, keeping the key content out of the top and bottom bands where the platform
    draws its own buttons and captions (check each platform's current safe-zone guide);
  - recompose each one; never crop UI so the key element falls outside.
- **The end card** holds for at least 3 s: the name, the ask and the address. One ask, never a menu of four.
- **Music** supports the pace and ducks under the voice. Sound effects are for clicks and arrivals, sparingly.

## 8. The voice

- **A confident colleague who uses the product, not an announcer.** Plain, warm and specific.
- Second person ("you"), present tense.
- Humour only if the brand has it, and never at the customer's expense.
- About 140 to 160 words a minute. Leave silence on the aha moment.
- Never "In today's fast-paced world", "Meet X, the all-in-one solution", "Say goodbye to", or a question the viewer
  has to answer.
- At most two rhetorical questions in a film, each answered at once ("Where did the afternoon go? Into copying
  numbers.").
- For an internal rollout: honest about the change, specific about what gets easier, clear about help. No selling
  words.

## 9. Review rubric (score before render; anything at 0 blocks)

| # | Check | 0 | 1 | 2 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | The first 3 s, muted, say who it is for or what they get | logo or title | vague | clear |
| 2 | The product is on screen by | after 30 s | 15 to 30 s | before 15 s (or the format justifies later) |
| 3 | Value moments | a feature tour | benefits said, not shown | benefit, feature, result for each |
| 4 | The aha moment | missing | shown in passing | shown, held, given silence |
| 5 | Every claim on screen is in the proof inventory with its source | no | mostly | yes |
| 6 | Comparisons, testimonials, logos, badges, AI, pricing, roadmap follow section 6 | a breach | unclear | clean |
| 7 | UI fidelity is faithful (no invented features or results) | no | simplified without labels where needed | yes |
| 8 | Sample data is invented, consistent and plausible | real or inconsistent | minor slips | clean |
| 9 | One ask, said and shown, with the end card held for at least 3 s | none or several | weak | clear |
| 10 | Words the viewer uses, no hype vocabulary | buzzwords | some | plain |
| 11 | Captions accurate, contrast readable, safe areas kept | no | partly | yes |
| 12 | Length fits the format | over by 30 % or more | over | fits |

**Failure modes, by name:**
- **feature tour:** every menu shown, nothing sold;
- **logo soup:** proof by quantity;
- **dashboard wallpaper:** a busy screen behind narration that never points at anything;
- **demo of everything:** the sales follow-up that shows all modules;
- **hype voice:** adjectives instead of evidence;
- **the slow hook:** five seconds of brand before a reason to watch;
- **the moving target:** the zoom that moves during the click;
- **the impossible screen:** a simplified UI showing something the product cannot do;
- **the ghost result:** the action is shown, the result is never held.

## 10. In Loom (when this skill runs inside the Video-Engine)

- **The kind of video is `product`** (`py -m loom new <id> --genre product`, or "auto" reads it from the topic). The
  profile (`loom/genre.py`):
  - no quiz, no recall and no check role;
  - the viewer may be addressed directly, including the ask ("try it free");
  - this skill leads pedagogy, script and storyboard. The arc is in `packs/genres/product.md`.
- **Ground it in the product's own material (source mode).** Feed the docs, the site pages, the release notes and
  approved case studies as sources.
  - In topic mode, the knowledge sheet becomes the product truth sheet, and the product owner approves it at the
    knowledge gate. Their name goes in `--by`.
  - The digits gate catches unsourced numbers; you catch the rest (section 6).
- **Styles** come from `py -m loom recommend-style --topic "<topic>" --kind product`. A style is chosen for the product, never
  ranked against another (D-122).
  - **screencast:** the product itself. The held shell, tour then value moments, `A.spotlight` on the aha,
    `A.timelapse` for waits, the five app shells (`app_email`, `app_calendar`, `app_chat_workspace`,
    `app_docs_editor`, `app_lms_course`) as a base.
    - The screencast card's "no real product's logo" rule protects generic lessons. For the owner's own product,
      use the owner's brand: a brand in `packs/brands/<id>/` (colours, fonts, logo; `py -m loom brand`), named
      in the storyboard's `brand`.
    - A captured real page needs approval for each site that is not on this machine.
  - **isometric:** the ecosystem or the journey of a request through the product (integrations, regions, the
    order's path).
  - **hologram:** AI, security and data products, where the subject cannot be seen. A hero is required (D-194).
  - **corporate-clean, glow-flat, presenter-pop:** homepage and launch explainers with simplified UI.
  - **story-cartoon:** the day-in-the-life before and after, with designed characters only.
- **The storyboard marks the cutdown beats** (`cut: [30, 15]` in the beat's `design` notes) and the end card hold.
  `py -m loom trailer <id>` cuts a 20 to 35 s version from the lesson's own scenes.
- **The critic's VIDEO KIND block points it at this skill:** judge with section 9's rubric, as well as the style.
- **A worked example** (a 90 s homepage film, beat by beat): `reference/worked-example.md`.

## Never

- invent a number, a customer, a quote, a logo, a badge, a result or a feature;
- show an unshipped feature as shipped, or a simplified screen that the product cannot produce;
- use real customer data on screen;
- copy another company's interface, logo, characters or video;
- end on more than one ask.
