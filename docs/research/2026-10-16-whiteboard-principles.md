Kshitij, 2026-10-16: "since you are in charge of the whiteboard animation system and style, look up this style and
animation videos and see if there are any guides that help people learn basic principles of this kind of video or
anyone has posted any analysis of any type that can help us improve this style."

Research is a reference, never a checklist (standing direction). Nothing was downloaded; pages were read only.
Marketing-agency blogs are rules of thumb, not evidence, and are marked as such.

## 1. What the learning research says (the strongest evidence)

| Source | What it tested | What it found | What it means for us |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Fiorella and Mayer (2016), *Journal of Educational Psychology* 108, 528-546; five experiments ([summary](https://www.learningscientists.org/blog/2017/1/24-1), [dissertation](https://alexandria.ucsb.edu/lib/ark:/48907/f37d2sbv)) | watching an instructor draw diagrams against seeing the same finished diagrams, with the narration the same | Drawing generally improved transfer. With only the hand visible, it helped learners of low and high prior knowledge alike. Drawing shown with NO hand was no better than the static diagram. With the whole body visible, only novices gained. | The hand is not decoration: in their data the benefit went away without it. Keep the one visible hand, drawing in sync with the words. Novices gain most, and our learners are corporate novices on most topics. |
| Schneider, Krieglstein, Beege and Rey (2023), *Heliyon*; two experiments, N = 133 ([record](https://doaj.org/article/b614400e1604410898273830657364b1)) | progressive drawing against a finished picture, with and without a narrative | Progressive drawing and a narrative context each supported learning: higher retention and transfer than the finished picture. | Drawing in step with the speech is the active part. A board that appears whole, or text that pops on, loses the effect. |
| A 2023 follow-up on the hand ([Springer, s40561-023-00258-6](https://link.springer.com/10.1186/s40561-023-00258-6)) | whether a visible hand helps | The paper calls the hand the style's most distinctive feature and tests it; its results could not be read here (behind sign-in). | Unread: do not cite its result. |
| Türkay (2016, N = 568, [Harvard VPAL](https://www.vpal.harvard.edu/publications/educational-impact-whiteboard-animations-experiment-using-popular-social-science)) and Türkay (2022, N = 299, [AJET](https://ajet.org.au/index.php/AJET/article/download/7639/1955/27989)) | whiteboard animation against slides, a staged lecture, audio and text, on social science lessons | Little or no evidence that the format alone beats the others on learning; some benefits on learner experience. The 2022 PDF's results could not be read here. | The look alone does not teach. What teaches is drawing in sync with speech, one idea at a time, with the hand guiding attention. Spend effort on sync and clarity, not ornament. |

The theory behind both lines of work: the drawing links each spoken idea to its place in the picture as it is said
(Mayer's temporal contiguity and signalling), and the hand is a cue for attention.

## 2. What practitioners say (craft)

**Andrew Park (Cognitive, RSA Animate), [Sketchnote Army interview](https://sketchnotearmy.com/blog/2025/11/18/andrew-park), 2025:**
- Audio first. He played a talk about 30 times and drew to its pauses and emphases. The audio decides when to draw,
  how fast the hand moves, and when to pop something on or slide the frame.
- He plans the large end state of the board first, then works backwards to the order of drawing. He leaves working
  space and off-screen areas the camera can cut to, and directs by deciding where to zoom in and out.
- Pictures with words: symbols, speech balloons, boxes, arrows, vignettes, metaphors, anthropomorphic objects, and
  compound images that join two unrelated ideas.
- Reuse: he rubs out one character and redraws it in a new state instead of redrawing a scene.
- Teaching films: an advance organiser to open, and a recap where the hand points to the key content.
- Imperfect marks are fine; red and black markers were what read best on camera.

**Rules of thumb from studios and tools** (vendor blogs: [Functioning Media](https://functioningmedia.substack.com/p/common-mistakes-to-avoid-when-creating), [TruScribe](https://truscribe.com/blog/whiteboard-video-hits-and-misses-making-the-right-choices-for-your-message/), [VideoScribe](https://blog.videoscribe.co/10-ways-to-make-a-winning-whiteboard-animation), [VideoScribe camera](https://help.videoscribe.co/knowledge/use-zoom-and-set-the-camera), [ATD](https://www.td.org/insights/toolbox-tip-creating-whiteboard-videos-with-videoscribe)):
- One main picture per idea or sentence; simple, iconic drawings; visual metaphors for abstract ideas.
- Crowded frames force the hand to draw frantically to keep up with the voice; a hand that speeds up in one paragraph
  and not the next feels uneven. Keep density controlled and the hand's pace consistent.
- Start drawing when the voice names the thing and finish as the point completes; pause briefly after complex ideas.
- Simple things fast, the key diagram slower for emphasis.
- One accent colour: several accents compete and the eye no longer knows what matters.
- If an element has too little time to be drawn, slide it in with the hand or let it appear, rather than scribble it
  unevenly.
- Do not open on slowly drawn text; win the viewer in the first 5 to 10 s.
- The camera: zoom and pan to focus on a detail and pull back for the big picture; a canvas larger than the frame; keep
  the camera consistent across a group of elements, and move it on purpose, not on every element.

## 4. What would raise the style most, in order

1. **Pictures, not only words:** a drawable icon and metaphor library: single-line centre-line SVG paths, labelled, in
   the style. The engine draws them stroke by stroke with the same hand. This is the gap the research and the
   practitioners both point at: one picture per idea.
2. **Camera follows the work:** engine-driven framing of the area being drawn, calm moves at idea boundaries, a pull-back
   at a section's end. It also makes a fuller board readable.
3. **Hand poses:** lifted, pointing (recap), placing (slide in), erasing. They need designed art in the same hand.
4. **Plan the section's finished board first**, then the drawing order (Park). A storyboard field and a gate.
5. **An even pace:** measure the hand's speed per beat and flag the outliers (TruScribe's uneven pacing).
6. **The sleeve's colour:** a neutral sleeve (ink or slate) so the accent is the only warm colour. It is Kshitij's hand,
   so this is his call.

## 5. Correction, same day: Lucide is the drawable icon set (Kshitij: "Are lucide icons not good for this?")

They are. Section 4's first item asked for a new set of about 150 centre-line icons; the repo already has 1,993 Lucide
icons (`assets/icons/lucide.json`, ISC, used by `kit.icon`), and every one is a centre-line stroke drawing (stroke
only, round caps, no fill): what the hand can trace. Tried in a scratch lab scene (not shipped, the engine untouched
while the layout guard trial runs): ten icons (lightbulb, target, clock, users, server, puzzle, scale, rocket, shield
check, git branch) traced by the browser (`getPointAtLength`), split where a path jumps between subpaths, and drawn by
the hand stroke by stroke in the engine's marker line. All ten drew cleanly and read at 220 px.
Limits seen: they are UI symbols, simple and uniform, so they read as icons, not illustrations; a stroke order is the
file's order, not always a person's; Lucide's dots (a 0.01 line, the server's lights) need a dot; brand marks must not
be used. What Lucide does not have is the compound metaphor and the corporate situation (a person juggling tasks, a
bridge between two teams, a funnel of requests): those are the only drawings to ask for, in the same form (stroke-only
SVG, round caps, no fill, labelled), so the same tracer draws them. Next: `W.icon(name, ...)` in the engine (after the
trial), with a drawing order (outline first, then the details) and dots.
