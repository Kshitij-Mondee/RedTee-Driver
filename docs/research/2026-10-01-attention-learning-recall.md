# Attention, Learning and Recall in Short Animated Learning Videos: What Recent Research Actually Says

Prepared for: the learning-video engine team (code-rendered flat, whiteboard, 2D character, 2.5D, isometric, simple 3D and Manim animation; synthesised music and sound; one TTS voice; on-screen captions).
Date of research: 1 October 2026.
Scope: evidence from 2018 to 2026 where it exists, with older work included only when it is foundational (marked "foundational").

---

## 0. How to read this report, and what was and was not verified

**Evidence labels used throughout**

- **Well established**: several meta-analyses or many independent replications, effect in a consistent direction, reasonable boundary conditions known.
- **Promising**: a meta-analysis with thin or mixed primary studies, or a handful of good experiments, or strong lab evidence not yet tested in workplace video.
- **Popular but weak or false**: widely repeated in L&D practice, with weak, absent, or contrary evidence.
- **Estimate**: a number I propose from judgement, not from a study. These are always labelled.

**Verification level of each source** (marked in the Sources list):

- **[read]**: I fetched and read the abstract or publisher page myself in this session.
- **[summary]**: I found the source and its key numbers through search results or secondary summaries, but did not read the paper or its abstract directly. Numbers from these are marked as such in the text when they carry weight.
- **[recollection]**: a foundational claim I state from background knowledge that I did not re-check in this session. Treat as unverified.

Blocked or unavailable: PubMed Central pages returned a browser check and were not read (not bypassed). Several paywalled full texts (Review of Educational Research, Psychological Bulletin, Computers & Education) were read only at abstract level. Where a finding rests on a moderator analysis inside a paper I could not open, I say so.

A general caution that applies to the whole field: most multimedia-learning experiments use university students, short lab sessions (under 20 minutes), and immediate tests. Very few measure retention after a week or more, and almost none measure on-the-job behaviour. Workplace L&D is a boundary condition for nearly everything below.

## 1. Executive summary

1. **The "goldfish" 8-second attention span is false**, and the "10 to 15 minute lecture attention limit" is not supported by primary data. Measured adult concentration on a standard attention test has, if anything, improved slightly since 1990. What varies is not a fixed attention span but whether the material gives the mind something to do.
2. **The single most powerful lever for attention and recall in video is making the learner retrieve or predict.** Interpolated questions roughly halved mind wandering in the classic study, and a 2025 meta-analysis of 54 video studies found active strategies (mostly embedded questions) improved retention, comprehension and transfer (g about 0.3 to 0.4). Spaced retrieval afterwards is the strongest known tool for long-term recall (g about 0.5 to 0.7).
3. **"Engaging" and "learns well" are not opposites.** Relevant engagement (emotional design, dynamic drawing, narrative structure, social cues, well-placed music, surprise that confronts a misconception) tends to help or be neutral. Irrelevant engagement (seductive details, decorative stories, stacked effects, music under dense narration) tends to hurt. The dividing line is relevance to the thing being learned, not the amount of polish.
4. **Motion is worth it when the content itself changes over time** (processes, procedures, cause and effect), and much less so otherwise. Animation beats static pictures only modestly on average (g about 0.2 to 0.4), with large gains for procedural and motor skills.
5. **Old principles mostly survive, with boundary conditions.** A 2022 meta-meta-analysis of 29 reviews (about 1,200 studies) found 11 multimedia principles hold up. But effects are smaller in real video than in lab text-and-picture materials, shrink for expert learners, and several (redundancy, modality, voice) have moved: modern TTS voices perform as well as human voices, and captions are more nuanced than "never duplicate narration".
6. **Short-form video habits correlate with worse attention and inhibitory control (r about -0.35 to -0.4), but the evidence is correlational**, and "brain rot" has not itself been studied. One experiment found learning from short social-style clips was worse than from equivalent text, and a 3-minute dose of entertainment clips beforehand encouraged shallower learning. Copying TikTok pacing into a learning video is not supported by evidence.
7. **AI-generated presenters produce the same test scores as human ones in several 2023 to 2025 experiments, but lower enjoyment.** Learning is not the bottleneck there; liking and trust are.

## 2. Findings by thread

Each finding gives: **Claim**, **Evidence**, **Strength and recency**, **Boundary conditions**, **For a 3 to 8 minute animated workplace video**.

### Thread 1. Attention in video

#### 1.1 Minds wander a lot during video, and it costs learning

- **Claim**: Learners are off-task for a large share of any educational activity, and off-task thought predicts worse learning.
- **Evidence**: Wong and colleagues (2022, Contemporary Educational Psychology) meta-analysed 130 samples: students were off-task about 30% of the time during educationally relevant activities, with a small-to-medium negative relationship to learning (reported as about -0.27) [summary]. In Szpunar, Khan and Schacter (2013, PNAS), students watching a 21-minute statistics video lecture without interpolated tests reported mind wandering about 40% of the time [summary; foundational].
- **Strength and recency**: Well established (large meta-analysis, 2022).
- **Boundary conditions**: Self-report probes; the relationship is correlational (weaker learners may wander more).
- **For our videos**: Assume that, by default, a learner is elsewhere for roughly a third of a video. Design so that the critical ideas are not delivered only once, only in narration, at a moment of low demand. Key points should be both said and shown, and recalled at least once before the end.

#### 1.2 Interpolated questions reduce mind wandering and improve learning

- **Claim**: Asking the learner to retrieve what they just saw, at intervals through a video, keeps attention on task and improves learning of both tested and later content.
- **Evidence**: Szpunar et al. (2013): tested students were about half as likely to mind wander, took about three times as many notes, retained more, and reported less anxiety about the final test [summary; foundational]. Jing, Szpunar and Schacter (2016): in a 40-minute video lecture, interpolated tests shifted thoughts toward the lecture and improved integration of ideas across segments [summary]. Szpunar, Jing and Schacter (2014): repeated interpolated tests corrected learners' overconfidence [summary]. Chan, Meissner and Davis (2018, Psychological Bulletin): retrieval of earlier material improves learning of new material ("test-potentiated new learning"), meta-analytic g about 0.44 [summary]. Zhang, Li, Pi and Yang (2025, Educational Research Review): 54 studies comparing active strategies with passive video watching (74% run 2019 to 2025, 93% adults, 75% STEM, embedded questions the most common strategy at 46%); retention g = 0.33, comprehension g = 0.28, transfer g = 0.43; motivation and cognitive load both rose [summary]. Frontiers 2025 search also surfaced a 237-student secondary-school study where interpolated questions in video modelling examples improved comprehension and application [summary].
- **Strength and recency**: Well established (2025 meta-analysis plus the classic replications).
- **Boundary conditions**: Almost all studies required learners to actually answer. There is little direct evidence that a rhetorical question posed in a linear MP4, where the learner does not respond, produces the same effect. Questions must target relevant content: a 2025 study found only content-relevant (episodic) retrieval enhanced new learning; autobiographical or general-knowledge questions did not [summary]. Cognitive load rises, so questions should be short.
- **For our videos**: Put a retrieval checkpoint at each segment boundary (roughly every 60 to 120 seconds of new content, estimate). In a linear MP4, show the question, give a visible 3 to 5 second think window with a countdown or "pause now" cue (estimate), then reveal and briefly explain the answer. Where the LMS or player allows it, make the response real (a click or typed answer), because that is what the evidence actually tested. Always pair the video with a short quiz afterwards.

#### 1.3 Prequestions before a segment help, but mostly for what they ask about

- **Claim**: Asking a question before the content is shown, even when learners get it wrong, focuses attention on that content.
- **Evidence**: Carpenter and Toftness (2017, Journal of Applied Research in Memory and Cognition): an Easter Island video split into three roughly 2-minute segments; learners who attempted two prequestions before each segment (and nearly always failed) outperformed controls on both prequestioned and new items, about 12% higher on non-prequestioned items [summary]. St. Hilaire, Chan and Ahn (2023 meta-analysis, as reported): prequestions give a moderate specific benefit for the asked-about content (g about 0.54) but a near-zero general benefit (g about 0.04) [summary]. Toftness and colleagues also published "The limited effects of prequestions on learning from authentic lecture videos" [summary; title only].
- **Strength and recency**: Promising to well established for the specific effect; weak for spill-over.
- **Boundary conditions**: Works for the questioned facts; do not expect it to lift everything.
- **For our videos**: Use prequestions (or a "predict first" prompt) for the one or two things that matter most in each segment, not as a generic hook.

#### 1.4 The "8-second goldfish attention span"

- **Claim**: Human attention spans have fallen to 8 seconds, below a goldfish.
- **Evidence**: The figure comes from a 2015 Microsoft Canada consumer-insights marketing report, which sourced it to "Statistic Brain", a website that provides no research behind it; no peer-reviewed study supports the human or goldfish figure [summary]. Bradbury (2016, Advances in Physiology Education) reviewed the evidence for a 10 to 15 minute lecture attention limit and found scant primary data, methodological flaws, and that the most-cited source barely discusses attention; variation between teachers mattered far more than format [summary]. Andrzejewski, Zeilinger and Pietschnig (2024, Personality and Individual Differences): cross-temporal meta-analysis of the d2 attention test, 287 samples, N = 21,291, 32 countries, 1990 to 2021: moderate generational gains in adult concentration performance; not in children, who showed more errors and more speed [summary].
- **Strength and recency**: The myth is **popular but false**. The debunking evidence is strong and recent.
- **Boundary conditions**: Sustained attention on a boring task does decline within minutes (vigilance decrement), and people do switch tasks more on screens. That is different from a fixed "span".
- **For our videos**: Do not design to a mythical 8-second clock (constant cuts, flashing). Design to give the mind a job every short while (predict, notice, compare, recall). Attention follows purpose, not a stopwatch.

#### 1.5 Video length and engagement drop-off

- **Claim**: Shorter videos are watched more completely.
- **Evidence**: Guo, Kim and Rubin (2014, ACM Learning at Scale): 6.9 million viewing sessions across 862 videos in four edX courses; shorter videos were much more engaging, informal and tablet-drawing styles were more engaging than polished studio lectures; the authors recommended segments under about 6 minutes [read abstract; the "6 minutes" recommendation confirmed via a secondary source; foundational]. Kim, Guo, Seaton and colleagues (2014): second-by-second analysis of the same data; longer videos had higher in-video dropout, and 61% of interaction peaks (replays, jumps) coincided with visual transitions [summary]. Easygenerator (2026, vendor platform data, 3,802 workplace courses, 730,747 learner results): completion 85.5% under 5 minutes, 81.5% at 5 to 15 minutes, 70.6% above 15 minutes; explicitly observational, and "completion is not learning" [read]. Wistia (vendor, 2024): engagement differs little between 1 and 5 minutes and falls for videos over 5 minutes [summary; vendor]. Counterpoint: Alvarado et al. (2023, Advances in Engineering Education): engineering students said they preferred short 4 to 12 minute videos but actually watched 30 to 60 minute videos more, because they wanted one sitting; learning differences inconclusive [read]. A randomised comparison of one 14-minute video versus three 4 to 5 minute segments found no learning difference [summary; source not fully identified]. Demir and Birgili (2024, n = 28): length did not change perceived learning, but learners reported focusing better on short videos [summary].
- **Strength and recency**: Well established that completion and watch-fraction fall with length (observational, large N). **Weak** that shorter videos cause better learning when the same content is covered.
- **Boundary conditions**: Engagement metrics are not learning. Mandatory compliance learners behave differently from voluntary MOOC viewers. Splitting content without adding checkpoints may not help learning at all.
- **For our videos**: The 3 to 8 minute target is well supported for completion. Inside it, what matters for learning is segment structure and retrieval, not raw length. If a topic needs 12 minutes, two 6-minute videos with a checkpoint between them are better than cutting content.

#### 1.6 Playback speed

- **Claim**: Watching faster costs little up to about 2x, at least on short-term tests.
- **Evidence**: Murphy, Hoover, Agadzhanyan, Kuehn and Castel (2022, Applied Cognitive Psychology): minimal cost from 1x to 1.5x or 2x, decline beyond 2x [summary]. Murphy et al. (2024, Educational Psychology Review), four experiments: 2.5x did not impair test performance; having visuals helped especially at speed; notifications and calls did not hurt at 1x or 2x [read]. Against this, Yang (2025, Frontiers in Psychology) meta-analysed 12 studies: acceleration increased cognitive load (g = 0.59) and lowered retention (g = -0.41), transfer (g = -0.50) and satisfaction (g = -0.24), with a warning about publication bias [read].
- **Strength and recency**: **Mixed** (two recent lines disagree). Probably: fluent adults tolerate speed for recall of simple lecture content; costs appear for transfer, for novices, and for dense material.
- **Boundary conditions**: Most studies used lecture recordings, not dense animation; tests were immediate.
- **For our videos**: Do not speed narration up to feel "modern". Keep a natural pace and let the player offer learner-controlled speed. Because visuals protect comprehension at speed, make sure each key point is visible, not only spoken.

#### 1.7 Visual density, not only visual change, drives pausing and dropout

- **Claim**: More simultaneous on-screen elements (especially text, formulas) are associated with more pauses, rewinds and dropouts.
- **Evidence**: A 2025 arXiv study (TIB Hannover) coded visual complexity in 25 physics and chemistry videos against real interaction logs: higher complexity, especially textual, correlated with more pauses, rewinds and session dropouts [read abstract]. Kim et al. (2014): interaction peaks cluster at visual transitions [summary].
- **Strength and recency**: Promising (observational, small video sample).
- **For our videos**: Build each frame up progressively rather than presenting a full dense slide. Put a checkpoint or a breath after dense frames, because that is where learners already try to pause.

#### 1.8 Short-form (TikTok-style) video as a learning format

- **Claim**: Social-media-style short clips are good at grabbing attention and poor at building understanding.
- **Evidence**: Otto and colleagues (TU Braunschweig, 2025, two studies in Computers & Education): about 170 adults, heavier short-video use associated with worse rational-thinking scores; about 120 adults aged 18 to 30, learning from two short videos was worse than from text with identical content, and watching 3 minutes of entertainment clips beforehand pushed learners toward a superficial approach [read press release]. Chiossi et al. (2023, CHI, N = 60): scrolling TikTok during a prospective memory task degraded performance, while Twitter, YouTube and no activity did not [read]. Engagement-style reports of short-video learning gains are mostly uncontrolled pre-post designs [summary].
- **Strength and recency**: Promising (recent, small, few experiments).
- **Boundary conditions**: "Short video" in these studies means fast, stimulus-dense, entertainment-styled clips, not a calm 3-minute explainer.
- **For our videos**: Borrow the good parts of short-form (a clear question in the first seconds, one idea per unit, tight editing) and not the bad parts (constant cuts, captions plus effects plus music plus fast talk all at once).

### Thread 2. Curiosity and surprise

#### 2.1 Information gaps create curiosity, and curiosity improves memory

- **Claim**: Curiosity arises when people notice a gap in something they partly know, and information that satisfies high curiosity is better remembered.
- **Evidence**: Loewenstein (1994) information-gap theory: curiosity needs some prior knowledge [summary; foundational]. Kang et al. (2009, Psychological Science): curiosity during trivia questions activated reward-related regions, and people paid scarce resources to learn answers they were curious about [summary; foundational]. Gruber, Gelman and Ranganath (2014, Neuron): better memory for curiosity-satisfying answers at immediate and 1-day tests, plus better memory for unrelated faces shown during high-curiosity states [summary; foundational]. Later work finds the target-memory benefit robust but the incidental-memory spill-over inconsistent [summary]. Murayama (2022, Psychological Review) reframes curiosity and interest as a reward-learning process that "self-boosts" over time [summary]. A study on hints found hints only raised curiosity when the content was otherwise boring [summary].
- **Strength and recency**: Well established for target information; promising for spill-over to nearby content.
- **Boundary conditions**: Mostly trivia questions in the lab. A gap only works if the learner already knows enough to feel it. Too big a gap produces confusion, not curiosity.
- **For our videos**: Open with a question the learner can almost answer, grounded in their work ("Two of these three invoices would pass audit. Which one fails?"), and answer it within the video. Avoid vague teasers ("You won't believe what happens next") that have no informational gap.

#### 2.2 Predict first: prediction plus surprise boosts learning

- **Claim**: Asking learners to commit to a prediction before seeing an outcome improves learning, and wrong predictions help most.
- **Evidence**: Crouch, Fagen, Callan and Mazur (2004, American Journal of Physics): students who passively watched demonstrations understood no better than those who did not see them (gain 0.09), predicting first raised the gain to 0.36, predicting plus discussing to 0.45, for about 2 extra minutes [summary; foundational]. Brod, Hasselhorn and Bunge (2018, Learning and Instruction): generating predictions beat post-hoc evaluation; pupil-measured surprise occurred only after a prediction and scaled with learning; false predictions drove much of the benefit [summary]. Brod et al. (2019, npj Science of Learning): generating a prediction also stimulates curiosity [summary]. A 2022 study reported a U-shaped relation between expectedness and memory, with pupil surprise to highly unexpected outcomes predicting learning [summary; authorship not verified]. An fMRI study (bioRxiv 2020 to 2021, published later) interrupted familiar narrative videos before expected endings and found prediction errors changed how the hippocampus stored and updated memories [summary].
- **Strength and recency**: Well established (classroom and lab, 2004 to 2022).
- **Boundary conditions**: The learner must actually commit (even privately). Children with weaker executive function benefit less from being proven wrong [summary].
- **For our videos**: "Pause and predict" is one of the best uses of a character scene: freeze just before a character's decision or a process outcome, ask what happens next, then reveal. Make the likely wrong answer tempting, so the surprise is informative.

#### 2.3 Confronting misconceptions beats clean exposition

- **Claim**: Stating a common misconception and then refuting it teaches more than a clear, correct explanation alone.
- **Evidence**: Muller, Bewes, Sharma and Reimann (2008): four versions of an online multimedia physics lesson; the refutation and dialogue versions produced the largest gains (effect sizes about 0.79 and 0.83 versus plain exposition), especially for low prior knowledge, without hurting high prior knowledge learners [summary; foundational]. Schroeder and Kucera (2022, Educational Psychology Review) refutation text meta-analysis, g about 0.41 [summary]; a larger preregistered meta-analysis (76 studies, 294 effect sizes) also found a consistent advantage [summary]. Hypercorrection: errors made with high confidence are corrected more readily, plausibly because the correction is surprising [summary; foundational]. Kulgemeyer and Wittwer (2022): an explainer video built on a misconception can feel just as clear as a correct one, producing an illusion of understanding [summary].
- **Strength and recency**: Well established for text; promising for video.
- **For our videos**: For topics with a known wrong belief (phishing "looks official so it's safe", "averages hide nothing"), make the misconception visible, let a character hold it, and show where it breaks. This is surprise that carries content.

#### 2.4 Surprise and joy hold attention in video

- **Claim**: Moments of surprise and joy concentrate gaze and keep viewers watching.
- **Evidence**: Teixeira, Wedel and Pieters (2012, Journal of Marketing Research): facial-expression coding and eye tracking on internet ads; surprise concentrated attention, rising joy reduced zapping [summary; marketing, foundational].
- **Strength and recency**: Promising (advertising context, older).
- **For our videos**: A small, relevant surprise at the 20 to 40% point of a video (estimate) can re-engage. It should reveal something about the content, not be a gag.

### Thread 3. Emotion and design

#### 3.1 Emotional design (warm colours, friendly faces on objects) helps modestly

- **Claim**: Adding pleasant colours and anthropomorphic faces to learning graphics improves learning a little.
- **Evidence**: Brom, Stárková and D'Mello (2018, Educational Research Review), 33 samples, N = 2,924: retention d = 0.39, comprehension d = 0.32, transfer d = 0.33, intrinsic motivation d = 0.26, lower perceived difficulty d = -0.21, but only d = 0.11 on positive affect [summary]. Wong and Adesope (2021, Educational Psychology Review), replication and extension: retention g = 0.35, transfer g = 0.27, comprehension g = 0.29; motivation effects stronger for static than animated materials [summary]. Lenski and Großschedl (2023, Frontiers in Psychology): emotional design stimuli were rated more pleasant; the title and design question whether they are strong enough to raise arousal or capture attention [summary]. Noetel et al. (2022) list anthropomorphics among robust principles [read].
- **Strength and recency**: Well established that it helps learning a little; weak that it works by changing mood.
- **Boundary conditions**: Effects smaller with animated materials; the mechanism is unclear (perhaps lower perceived difficulty rather than emotion).
- **For our videos**: Warm, coherent palettes and friendly faces on key objects (a padlock with eyes, a smiling data point) are low-cost and slightly positive. Do not expect them to rescue a dull script.

#### 3.2 Seductive details usually hurt

- **Claim**: Interesting but irrelevant additions (fun facts, decorative anecdotes, unrelated images) reduce learning.
- **Evidence**: Rey (2012) meta-analysis found negative effects on recall and transfer, stronger for visual seductive details [summary; foundational]. Sundararajan and Adesope (2020, Educational Psychology Review): 58 studies, 68 effects, over 7,500 learners, g = -0.33; larger harm in some formats and placements (static images, text and image; one subgroup around g = -0.70) and in paper versus digital materials [summary; moderator details not verified]. Eitel, Bender and Renkl (2019): whether a detail seduces may depend on whether learners believe it is relevant [summary]. Noetel et al. (2022): removing seductive details is among the robust principles [read].
- **Strength and recency**: Well established.
- **Boundary conditions**: Harm is smaller for short video details than static images in the 2020 meta-analysis [summary]; emotional benefits may partly compensate but this is not established.
- **For our videos**: Apply a deletion test to every element: "If I cut this, does the learner lose a step of understanding?" If not, cut it or make it carry content. A joke or story is fine if it is the example.

#### 3.3 Humour

- **Claim**: Humour improves how learners rate the instruction, but reliably improves learning only when it is about the content.
- **Evidence**: Banas, Dunbar, Rodriguez and Liu (2011) review: appropriate humour improves evaluations and climate, evidence on learning is mixed [summary; foundational]. Zhou and Lee (2025, Frontiers in Psychology) five-decade review: related, appropriate humour tends to help; inconsistent findings overall; Instructional Humor Processing Theory predicts benefits only when humour is recognised, understood, appropriate and related [summary].
- **Strength and recency**: Promising for related humour; **popular but weak** for humour as a general engagement tool.
- **For our videos**: Humour should be the example (a character making the exact mistake the lesson is about), not decoration. Avoid humour in compliance topics where it can trivialise harm.

#### 3.4 Music in instructional video

- **Claim**: Background music during narration can hurt verbal recall; music elsewhere is neutral to helpful.
- **Evidence**: Moreno and Mayer (2000): adding music and sounds to a narrated animation reduced recall and transfer [summary; foundational]. Bai (2023, Psychology of Music) meta-analysis of 47 studies, 71 effects: small positive mean effect of background music on learning (d = 0.31), medium when music preceded the assessment, positive for factual retention and for classical music, with age as a moderator; the authors argue this contradicts blanket advice against music [read abstract]. Amato (2026, Cultura e Saude, single-author review in a small general-interest journal): 13 reports, 26 effects; music raised attentional engagement strongly (g = 1.06) and the memory penalty appeared only when music overlapped narration (g = -0.46), near zero otherwise (g = -0.13) [read; low-weight venue, not independently checked].
- **Strength and recency**: Mixed. Well established that concurrent music plus dense narration is a risk (the one condition with consistent cost). Promising that music is neutral or positive elsewhere.
- **For our videos**: Use music for openings, transitions, section stings, think-pauses and the close. Duck it well under narration or drop it during dense explanation. Choose calm, low-lyric, low-complexity beds.

#### 3.5 Positive instructor emotion and conversational style

- **Claim**: Learners detect an instructor's emotional tone, and positive, conversational delivery is slightly better than flat, formal delivery.
- **Evidence**: Lawson, Mayer and colleagues (2021, "The positivity principle", ETR&D): learners recognised happy, content, frustrated and bored instructors in video lectures [summary; learning effects not verified by me]. Horovitz and Mayer (2021): studied human and virtual instructors showing happy or bored emotions [title verified via reference list; findings not verified]. Personalisation (using "you", conversational style) is supported by meta-analyses, including Liu et al. (2023, self-referencing, small to moderate effect) [summary]; but Almeida et al. (2024, Journal of Computer Assisted Learning) titled their result "More personal but not better" [summary].
- **Strength and recency**: Promising, with some recent null results.
- **For our videos**: Write narration in second person, active voice, short sentences. Choose a TTS style with warmth and moderate energy. Do not expect large learning gains from tone alone.

### Thread 4. Narrative and characters

#### 4.1 Stories are remembered and understood better than exposition

- **Claim**: The same information told as a narrative is better recalled and understood than as expository text.
- **Evidence**: Mar, Li, Nguyen and Ta (2021, Psychonomic Bulletin & Review): over 75 samples and 33,000 participants; narratives were more easily understood and better recalled than essays, robust to single studies and not moderated by study features [summary]. A science-education meta-analysis (30 studies, 72 comparisons) found a small benefit of narrative instruction (reported as g = 0.16) [summary; source and figure not verified directly]. A 2025 Instructional Science experiment (109 biology undergraduates, co-authored by M. Kapur) found narratives improved transfer, with tentative evidence that low prior knowledge learners benefit more and high prior knowledge learners do better with exposition [read abstract].
- **Strength and recency**: Well established for text; promising for video and workplace.
- **Boundary conditions**: Experts may gain little or lose. Narrative must carry the content (the story is the example), not wrap it.
- **For our videos**: Use a single workplace scenario as the spine: a character, a goal, a complication that the concept resolves, a decision, a consequence. Keep the story no bigger than the concept needs.

#### 4.2 Pedagogical agents and characters: small benefit, simpler is better

- **Claim**: On-screen agents slightly improve learning; simpler 2D agents tend to do better than realistic 3D ones.
- **Evidence**: Castro-Alonso, Wong, Adesope and Paas (2021, Educational Psychology Review): 32 effects, N = 2,104, g = 0.20 overall; 2D agents g = 0.38 versus 3D agents g = 0.11; nonverbal communication, motion and voice did not moderate effectiveness [summary]. Davis (2018, Educational Research Review): agents that gesture help more than static agents [summary]. Wang and Luo (2026, Frontiers in Psychology, 40 studies, N = 5,049): social cues (teaching agents, gestures) g = 0.63 versus visual cues g = 0.26; combining social and visual cues showed a negative effect (g = -0.64, only 8 studies) [read].
- **Strength and recency**: Promising to well established (small but consistent).
- **Boundary conditions**: Agents must direct attention to content (gaze, pointing), not compete with it.
- **For our videos**: A flat 2D character that looks at and points to the thing being explained is the evidence-friendly choice. Realistic 3D humans add cost and uncanny risk without learning benefit.

#### 4.3 Instructor presence: motivation yes, learning not reliably

- **Claim**: Showing a presenter raises liking and motivation but does not reliably improve learning, and adds load.
- **Evidence**: Alemdag (2022, Education and Information Technologies), 20 experiments: presence did not affect learning or social presence, but increased motivation and cognitive load [summary]. Henderson and Schroeder (2021): no clear evidence for or against [summary]. Beege, Schroeder, Heidig, Rey and Schneider (2023, Educational Research Review), 35 studies: positive effects on motivation and emotion ratings, not on learning; eye-tracking studies show learners spend a sizeable share of time (about 30%, per a secondary summary) looking at the instructor [summary].
- **Strength and recency**: Well established (several recent meta-analyses agree).
- **For our videos**: A presenter character is a motivational device, not a learning device. Keep it small, off to the side, and absent during dense explanation. Let the hand or pointer be the "presence".

#### 4.4 Watching it being drawn helps

- **Claim**: Content drawn dynamically in time with narration teaches better than static finished drawings.
- **Evidence**: Fiorella, Stull, Kuhlmann and Mayer (2019, Journal of Educational Psychology): dynamic drawings outperformed static drawings (d = 0.54); an instructor who kept eye contact via a transparent board outperformed one facing away (d = 0.54) [summary]. Hasso et al. (2025, Smart Learning Environments, n = 521): whiteboard animation with narration beat slideshow with narration, narration only and text only on affective and retention outcomes, with retention differences persisting one week [read]. Türkay (2016, Computers & Education): whiteboard animations improved retention and enjoyment versus other formats [summary]. Guo et al. (2014): Khan-style tablet drawings were more engaging in MOOCs [read].
- **Strength and recency**: Promising to well established (several independent studies, 2016 to 2025).
- **Boundary conditions**: Drawing speed must match narration (temporal contiguity). Drawing everything slowly wastes time; draw the new element, not the scaffolding.
- **For our videos**: This is a strength of a code-rendered whiteboard style: reveal each element as it is named. It is both "interesting" and evidence-based.

#### 4.5 AI-generated instructors and avatars (2023 to 2026)

- **Claim**: AI-generated presenters produce similar learning to human presenters, but lower enjoyment and engagement.
- **Evidence**: Leiker, Gyllen, Eldesouky and Cukurova (2023, AIED, n = 83 adults): no difference in learning gains or perceptions between a traditional instructor video and one with a realistic AI-generated presenter [summary]. Arkün-Kocadere and Çağlar Özhan (2024, IRRODL, n = 108): lower video engagement with the AI instructor, no difference in academic performance; students described distraction, discomfort and disconnection that faded when the topic was interesting [read]. Netland et al. (2025, Computers & Education, n = 447): same exam results, students enjoyed human-made videos more [summary]. A reported finding that disclosing an avatar's synthetic nature mid-video lowers trust [summary; primary source not identified, unverified].
- **Strength and recency**: Promising (several recent experiments, all short-term).
- **For our videos**: The engine does not need a photoreal avatar. A stylised character plus a good voice avoids the discomfort these studies report. Be transparent that narration is synthetic where policy requires.

#### 4.6 The voice: modern TTS is fine

- **Claim**: The old "voice principle" (human voice beats machine voice) no longer holds for modern synthetic voices.
- **Evidence**: Craig and Schroeder (2017): a modern TTS engine matched or beat a human voice on transfer and credibility ratings, while an old engine lagged [summary]. Craig and Schroeder (2019, Journal of Educational Computing Research): follow-up without a visible agent [summary]. Chiou, Schroeder and Craig (2020, Computers & Education) studied how voice quality affects trust and learning with virtual humans [title verified via reference list; findings not verified].
- **Strength and recency**: Promising (a few studies; TTS has improved further since).
- **For our videos**: One good modern voice is not a handicap. Invest in prosody (emphasis, pauses at boundaries, natural rate) rather than switching to human recording.

### Thread 5. Memory and recall

#### 5.1 Retrieval practice (the testing effect)

- **Claim**: Trying to recall information improves long-term memory more than re-watching or re-reading.
- **Evidence**: Adesope, Trevisan and Sundararajan (2017, Review of Educational Research): 188 articles, 272 effects; practice tests beat restudy and other comparisons, largest with 1 to 6 day lags, and benefits held for retention and transfer [summary]. Yang et al. (2021, Psychological Bulletin): 222 classroom studies, 48,478 students, g = 0.50; moderated by feedback, repetitions, and match between practice and final test format [summary]. Donoghue and Hattie (2021, Frontiers in Education) meta-analysis of ten learning techniques: distributed practice and practice testing were the most effective [summary].
- **Strength and recency**: Well established.
- **For our videos**: The video is the first exposure; the learning happens in the recall that follows. Every video should ship with 3 to 6 retrieval questions (estimate) that match the job use of the knowledge.

#### 5.2 Spacing

- **Claim**: Spreading retrieval over days and weeks beats massing it, and the ideal gap grows with how long you need to remember.
- **Evidence**: Latimier, Peyre and Ramus (2021, Educational Psychology Review): 29 studies; spaced beats massed retrieval practice (g = 0.74); no clear advantage of expanding over uniform schedules [summary]. Cepeda et al. (2008, Psychological Science, over 1,350 learners): optimal gap about 20 to 40% of a 1-week retention interval, falling to about 5 to 10% of a 1-year interval [summary; foundational]. Kim, Wong-Kee-You, Wiseheart and Rosenbaum (2019, Behavior Research Methods): 10,514 people in real workplace training; the optimal spacing between retrieval events increased with retention interval, as in the lab [summary]. Kerfoot et al. (2009): spaced online questions for 537 urology residents; advantage still present after 2 years (effect size 0.35) [summary; foundational]. A further meta-analysis (22 reports, 31 effects) found d = 0.54 for distributed over massed practice [summary].
- **Strength and recency**: Well established, including in workplace data.
- **For our videos**: A single 5-minute video cannot deliver spacing. Plan a follow-up: 2 or 3 short retrieval prompts (or a 60-second recap clip with questions) at roughly 2 days, 1 to 2 weeks, and 1 month after (estimate derived from Cepeda's ratios for a months-long retention need).

#### 5.3 Generative activities: explain, predict, draw, compare

- **Claim**: Activities that make learners produce something (explanations, predictions, drawings, comparisons) improve understanding.
- **Evidence**: Brod (2021, Educational Psychology Review) review of six generative strategies: all effective for university students; mixed for younger learners [summary]. Bisra et al. (2018): self-explanation prompts g = 0.55 across 69 effects, not explained by time on task [summary]. Fernandes, Wammes and Meade (2018, Current Directions): drawing to-be-remembered items beats writing, visualising or viewing pictures [summary]. Alfieri, Nokes-Malach and Schunn (2013, Educational Psychologist): comparing cases improves learning (d about 0.50) [summary; foundational].
- **Strength and recency**: Well established.
- **For our videos**: Use side-by-side comparisons (good versus bad email, compliant versus non-compliant form) and prompts like "Say in one sentence why the second one fails" before revealing the answer.

#### 5.4 Distinctiveness and the von Restorff effect

- **Claim**: An item that stands out from its surroundings is remembered better.
- **Evidence**: von Restorff (1933) and a 2020 historical review (MacLeod, Memory & Cognition) [summary; foundational]. The effect depends on contrast with context: distinctiveness only works if most things are not distinctive [recollection; general principle of the isolation effect].
- **Strength and recency**: Well established in lists; weakly tested in instructional video.
- **For our videos**: Reserve one visual treatment (a colour, a sound sting, a zoom) for the single most important idea per segment. If every point gets the effect, none stands out.

#### 5.5 Dual coding and the multimedia principle

- **Claim**: Words plus relevant pictures beat words alone.
- **Evidence**: Noetel et al. (2022) confirm the multimedia principle among robust effects [read]. Picture superiority is well replicated [summary].
- **Strength and recency**: Well established. Note: dual coding is not "learning styles".
- **For our videos**: Every narrated idea should have a visual that shows the idea's structure (a diagram, a relationship, a before and after), not a stock illustration of the topic.

#### 5.6 Microlearning

- **Claim**: Bite-sized learning is more effective.
- **Evidence**: Monib, Qazi and Apong (2025, Heliyon) systematic review of 40 studies: generally positive outcomes, proposes a design framework [summary]. Reviews note microlearning is inconsistently defined and most studies measure satisfaction or engagement rather than robust learning or transfer [summary]. A 2024 meta-analysis in a small regional journal pooled only 654 students (mean difference 12.6 points, wide CI) [summary; weak].
- **Strength and recency**: **Popular but weak** as a proven method; the ingredients that work inside it (segmenting, spacing, retrieval) are well established on their own.
- **For our videos**: "Micro" is not the active ingredient. Short units help completion and make spacing easier; retrieval and spacing are what produce recall.

#### 5.7 Feedback on checks

- **Claim**: Feedback that explains why an answer is right or wrong beats simple right or wrong feedback.
- **Evidence**: A meta-analysis of feedback in computer-based learning found knowledge-of-response feedback mostly ineffective, correct-answer feedback moderately helpful for lower-order outcomes, and elaborated feedback helpful for both lower and higher order outcomes [summary; I believe this is Van der Kleij, Feskens and Eggen 2015, not verified]. Correct-answer feedback on multiple choice can occasionally be over-applied to related questions [summary].
- **Strength and recency**: Well established.
- **For our videos**: After each checkpoint reveal, give one sentence of "because", ideally visual.

### Thread 6. Motion and visuals

#### 6.1 Animation versus static pictures

- **Claim**: Animation beats static graphics only modestly on average, and strongly when the change itself is what must be learned.
- **Evidence**: Höffler and Leutner (2007): d = 0.37 overall; d = 1.06 for procedural-motor knowledge; d = 0.76 for realistic (video-based) animation [summary; foundational]. Berney and Bétrancourt (2016, Computers & Education): 61 studies, 140 comparisons, g = 0.23; higher when system-paced (g = 0.31), with narration (g = 0.34), and without accompanying text (g = 0.88) [summary]. Ploetzner, Berney and Bétrancourt (2020 to 2021): the benefit of animation unfolds when the features of change need to be learned [summary]. Noetel et al. (2022) list animation among robust effects [read]. The transient information effect: long, continuous animations lose information before it can be processed, especially for complex material [summary; foundational: Leahy and Sweller 2011].
- **Strength and recency**: Well established.
- **For our videos**: Animate processes, sequences, flows, cause and effect, and procedures. Hold still for definitions, lists and comparisons. Break long animations into short segments with holds.

#### 6.2 Signalling and cueing

- **Claim**: Cues (highlights, arrows, colour, zooms, gestures) that point to what matters improve learning.
- **Evidence**: Schneider, Beege, Nebel and Rey (2018, Educational Research Review): strong effect on retention, moderate on transfer, and lower perceived difficulty [summary]. Alpizar, Adesope and Wong (2020, ETR&D) meta-analysis of signalling [summary; effect size not captured]. Noetel et al. (2022): signalling among the largest benefits [read]. Wang and Luo (2026): overall g = 0.32; smaller for instructional videos (g = 0.17) and not significant for animations (g = 0.07); combined social plus visual cues negative (k = 8) [read]. Eye-tracking work (de Koning et al.; Boucheix and Lowe) shows cues reliably steer gaze but do not always improve comprehension [summary].
- **Strength and recency**: Well established for static materials; **smaller and less certain in animation and video**.
- **Boundary conditions**: Cues select; they do not explain. In video, where motion already draws the eye, extra cues add less and can clutter.
- **For our videos**: One cue at a time, timed to the narration word. Use gaze and pointing of a character, or a single highlight, not arrow plus glow plus zoom plus sound together.

#### 6.3 Segmenting and pacing

- **Claim**: Breaking instruction into meaningful segments improves learning, even when the system (not the learner) sets the pause.
- **Evidence**: Rey et al. (2019, Educational Psychology Review): 56 investigations, 88 comparisons; small to medium effects on retention and transfer; reduced cognitive load and longer learning time; effects confirmed for system-paced segmentation; learners with high prior knowledge benefited more on retention [summary]. Noetel et al. (2022): good design matters more in system-paced formats like lectures and video than in self-paced ones [read].
- **Strength and recency**: Well established.
- **For our videos**: A linear MP4 is system-paced, so segmenting matters more for us than for a website. End each segment with a visible boundary: a short hold, a recap line, a music sting, a checkpoint.

#### 6.4 Event boundaries and cuts

- **Claim**: Memory is organised around event boundaries; cuts help when they coincide with a real change of scene or activity.
- **Evidence**: Event segmentation research: film cuts are not perceived as boundaries unless they coincide with a change in scene or activity, and order memory was better when cuts coincided with event boundaries; cueing boundaries improved memory in younger and older adults [summary]. Kim et al. (2014): interaction peaks at visual transitions [summary].
- **Strength and recency**: Promising (lab, general memory, not instructional-specific).
- **For our videos**: Make every scene change mean something (new idea, new step, new character decision). Avoid cuts inside a single idea just to "keep it moving".

#### 6.5 Text on screen, narration and captions (redundancy)

- **Claim**: Full on-screen text that duplicates narration usually hurts when there are also graphics, but short labels help, and captions are more nuanced than the old rule.
- **Evidence**: Mayer and Fiorella (2021, Cambridge Handbook) redundancy boundary conditions: on-screen text alongside narration can help when there are no graphics, when pace is slow or learner-controlled, when narration has technical or unfamiliar words, and when the text is much shorter than the narration (key words placed next to the graphic) [summary]. A 2016 meta-analysis of the modality effect (91 studies): narration beats on-screen text (d about 0.24 to 0.25), mainly with system-paced, dynamic, short materials; self-paced conditions can reverse it [summary]. Evans and Wang (2021, conference paper, UCL): captions reduced understanding in some conditions of a narrated demonstration [read]. Gernsbacher (2015): over 100 studies show captions improve comprehension, attention and memory for video, especially for second-language viewers, developing readers and D/deaf viewers [summary]. Noetel et al. (2022): captioning second-language videos is among the largest benefits [read]. Lopukhina et al. (2025, Psychological Science, 180 children): whether viewers read subtitles depends on reading fluency [read]. Otto (2025) advises avoiding subtitles in short videos as extra load [read; press release opinion].
- **Strength and recency**: Well established for the general redundancy effect with graphics; **mixed** for same-language captions for native speakers.
- **Boundary conditions**: Accessibility requires captions to exist; the design question is whether they are on by default and how they are styled.
- **For our videos**: Provide captions (accessibility, second-language staff, sound-off viewing), but make them toggleable, and keep them out of the region where the diagram lives. Keep on-screen text as short labels synced to the words, not sentences that repeat narration. For multilingual workforces, captions are likely a net positive.

#### 6.6 Realism versus schematic visuals

- **Claim**: Schematic visuals are better for understanding abstract relations; realism helps when learners must recognise real things.
- **Evidence**: Skulmowski, Nebel, Remmele and Rey (2022, Educational Psychology Review) review: realistic detail does not help or hinders abstract comprehension and inference, but can help learning concrete visual information, particularly when the test uses realistic images and for high spatial ability learners [summary].
- **Strength and recency**: Promising.
- **For our videos**: Flat, schematic styles suit concepts and processes. Use realistic screenshots or rendered objects when the job requires recognising the real thing (a UI, a hazard label, a machine part).

### Thread 7. Critiques and updates of Mayer's principles and cognitive load theory

#### 7.1 The principles mostly hold up, with smaller effects and clear boundaries

- **Evidence**: Noetel et al. (2022, Review of Educational Research): 29 reviews, 1,189 studies, 78,177 participants; 11 principles with significant positive effects on learning (largest for L2 captioning, contiguity and signalling; robust for modality, animation, coherence, anthropomorphics, segmentation, personalisation, pedagogical agents and verbal redundancy); good design matters more for complex material and system-paced formats [read]. A search summary attributes to a multilevel analysis of these principles an overall g = 0.37 and a small decline in effect size per year [summary; attribution not verified]. Mayer (2024, Educational Psychology Review) now lists 15 principles and calls for identifying boundary conditions [summary].
- **Strength**: Well established.

#### 7.2 Replication, measurement and theory critiques

- **Evidence**: Sweller (2023, Educational Psychology Review): cognitive load theory has repeatedly expanded because of replication failures, which revealed boundary conditions [summary]. de Jong (2010) critiqued CLT concepts and cognitive-load measures [summary; foundational]. The modality effect had a documented double replication failure (Lindow et al., 2011) [summary]. Martella, Lawson and Robinson (2024, Education Sciences): CLT studies in six journals across 2020 and 2023 mostly used rigorous designs [read summary blog].
- **What this means**: Treat the principles as defaults with known exceptions, not laws.

#### 7.3 Expertise reversal

- **Evidence**: Guidance that helps novices can hinder experts; supported with a large overall effect across studies [summary; foundational: Kalyuga and colleagues]. Echoed in Wang and Luo (2026), where cues helped undergraduates but not mixed graduate samples [read], and in narrative research (Section 4.1).
- **For our videos**: For expert audiences, cut scaffolding (fewer cues, less narration of the obvious, faster), and let them skip to checks.

#### 7.4 Where "engaging" additions were found to help rather than hurt

- Emotional design (colour, faces): small positive effects on learning (Section 3.1).
- Dynamic drawing and whiteboard animation: positive (Section 4.4).
- Narrative framing: positive, especially for novices (Section 4.1).
- Social cues and gesturing agents: positive (Section 4.2).
- Background music not overlapping narration: neutral to positive (Section 3.4).
- Related humour: possibly positive (Section 3.3).
- Refutation and surprise tied to content: positive (Sections 2.2 and 2.3).

And where they hurt: irrelevant seductive details (3.2), stacked cues (6.2), dense simultaneous stimuli in short social clips (1.8), music under dense narration (3.4), instructor video adding load without learning (4.3).

#### 7.5 Fluency illusions: polish can fool everyone

- **Evidence**: Carpenter, Wilford, Kornell and Mullaney (2013): a fluent, confident instructor raised learners' ratings and predicted learning but not actual learning [summary; foundational]. Toftness et al. (2018, Metacognition and Learning) replicated this [summary]. Perceptual disfluency (hard-to-read fonts) does not help either: Xie, Zhou and Liu (2018) meta-analysis found a null effect (d about 0.01) [summary].
- **For our videos**: Do not judge a lesson by how good it feels to watch. Judge by check-question performance, ideally delayed.

### Thread 8. Modern media habits, short video exposure, "brain rot"

- **Claim**: Heavy short-form video use is linked to worse attention and self-control; whether it causes it is unknown.
- **Evidence**: Psychological Bulletin (2025, vol. 151(9), "Feeds, feelings, and focus"; first author reported as Nguyen): 71 studies, 98,299 participants; more short-form video use associated with poorer cognition (r = -0.34), attention (r = -0.38) and inhibitory control (r = -0.41), and with stress and anxiety; consistent across youth and adults; few studies of memory or reasoning [read abstract]. Press summaries report that compulsive use mattered more than minutes watched [summary; not verified in the paper]. A 2025 review in Brain Sciences states that "brain rot" itself has not been systematically studied [summary]. Experimental: Chiossi et al. (2023) prospective memory drop with TikTok-style context switching [read]; Otto et al. (2025) brief exposure encourages superficial learning [read]. Counter-evidence: adult concentration test scores have risen since 1990 (Andrzejewski et al., 2024) [summary]. Child studies (Lillard and Peterson 2011; Lillard et al. 2015) found short-term executive function dips after fantastical, fast shows in 4 to 6 year olds, with fantasy rather than pacing driving the effect in one study [summary; foundational, children only].
- **Strength and recency**: Promising and recent for an association; causal claims are **unproven**. "Brain rot" as a clinical or cognitive construct is **popular but unstudied**.
- **For our videos**: Do not assume learners "can only handle" TikTok pacing. Do assume they arrive from high-switching contexts: give a clear orienting purpose in the first seconds, and a calmer, single-stream pace inside.

## 3. Evidence tiers at a glance

### Well established

- Retrieval practice improves long-term memory (Adesope 2017; Yang 2021).
- Spacing retrieval beats massing it; optimal gap scales with retention need (Latimier 2021; Cepeda 2008; Kim 2019 workplace).
- Embedded or interpolated questions in video improve learning and reduce mind wandering (Szpunar 2013; Zhang 2025).
- Predict-then-observe beats passive observation (Crouch 2004; Brod 2018).
- Refuting misconceptions beats plain exposition (Muller 2008; refutation meta-analyses).
- Removing irrelevant seductive details improves learning (Rey 2012; Sundararajan 2020).
- Segmenting helps, especially in system-paced video (Rey 2019).
- Signalling helps, most clearly in static materials (Schneider 2018; Noetel 2022).
- Animation helps most when change itself must be learned (Höffler 2007; Berney 2016; Ploetzner 2020).
- Narration plus relevant graphics beats on-screen text plus graphics for short, system-paced material (modality meta-analysis; Noetel 2022).
- Stories are better recalled than exposition (Mar 2021).
- Instructor presence raises motivation but not learning (Alemdag 2022; Beege 2023).
- Fluent delivery inflates perceived learning without raising actual learning (Carpenter 2013).
- Longer videos are watched less completely (Guo 2014; platform data).

### Promising

- Emotional design (pleasant colour, faces) gives small learning gains (Brom 2018; Wong 2021); mechanism unclear.
- Dynamic drawing and whiteboard animation (Fiorella 2019; Hasso 2025).
- 2D gesturing agents and social cues (Castro-Alonso 2021; Wang 2026).
- Modern TTS equals human voice (Craig and Schroeder 2017, 2019).
- AI presenters give similar learning with lower enjoyment (Leiker 2023; Arkün-Kocadere 2024; Netland 2025).
- Background music outside narration is neutral to positive (Bai 2023; Amato 2026 low weight).
- Curiosity improves memory for target content; spill-over to nearby content is inconsistent (Gruber 2014 and later work).
- Captions help second-language viewers strongly and native viewers sometimes (Gernsbacher 2015; Noetel 2022).
- Short-form social video learning is worse than text for low-complexity content (Otto 2025).
- Visual density predicts pausing and dropout (2025 log study).

### Popular but weak or false

- **8-second goldfish attention span**: false (no source).
- **10 to 15 minute attention limit in lectures**: unsupported (Bradbury 2016).
- **Attention spans are collapsing**: adult test performance has improved (Andrzejewski 2024).
- **Learning styles matching**: tiny, rare, low-quality effects; not worth adopting (Clinton-Lisell and Litzinger 2024: g = 0.31 but only 26% of outcomes show the required crossover).
- **Microlearning is inherently more effective**: definitional mess; benefits come from segmenting, spacing and retrieval.
- **Humour makes people learn more**: only when related to content; mostly boosts ratings.
- **Faster cuts and more stimulation hold attention better for learning**: not supported; dense multi-stream stimulation is associated with shallower learning.
- **Harder-to-read presentation deepens processing**: null (Xie 2018).
- **Human voice always beats synthetic**: outdated for modern TTS.
- **"Brain rot" is a proven cognitive effect**: unstudied as such; associations are correlational.
- **Completion rate and likes measure learning**: they do not (Easygenerator's own caveat; fluency research).

## 4. Synthesis for the team

### 4.1 Fourteen ideas for judgement (not a checklist)

Each idea: what it looks like in an animated lesson, and when it does not apply.

1. **Give the mind a job every short while.** Attention is not a fixed span; it follows purpose. In practice: every 60 to 120 seconds (estimate), the learner is asked to predict, notice, choose or recall something. *Does not apply* as strongly to a 60-second single-concept clip, where one opening question may be enough.

2. **Open with a real gap, not a teaser.** A question the learner can almost answer, rooted in their job, answered within the video. In practice: "Three expense claims. One breaks policy. Which?" shown in the first 5 to 10 seconds (estimate). *Does not apply* when learners have zero prior knowledge: then give a 15-second orienting example first, because curiosity needs something to hang on.

3. **Make surprise carry content.** The best surprise is a common misconception being overturned. In practice: a character confidently does the plausible wrong thing; freeze; ask; reveal consequence and reason. *Does not apply* where there is no real misconception; manufactured twists are seductive details.

4. **Retrieval is the lesson's second half, not an optional quiz.** In practice: end-of-segment checkpoints inside the video (question, think window, reveal with "because"), plus 3 to 6 questions after, plus spaced follow-ups days and weeks later. *Caveat*: in a pure linear MP4 without responses, treat checkpoints as prompts whose effect is not well evidenced; pair them with a real response wherever the platform allows.

5. **Animate change, hold still for structure.** Motion earns its place when the thing itself moves, flows, grows or happens in sequence. In practice: animate a data flow, a procedure, a causal chain; hold a definition or a comparison still while it is read and heard. *Does not apply* to purely motivational openings, where motion is a hook, not content.

6. **Draw it as you say it.** Reveal each element at the moment it is named (contiguity plus dynamic drawing). In practice: whiteboard-style strokes or progressive builds synced to narration words. *Does not apply* to elements the learner already knows (draw the new thing, not the scaffolding), or to experts who need speed.

7. **One cue at a time, timed to the word.** Pointing, highlighting and zooming help selection, but stacking them backfires, and motion already pulls the eye. In practice: a character glance plus pointer, or a single highlight, never arrow plus glow plus zoom plus sound. *Does not apply* in dense static diagrams, where a stronger signal is justified.

8. **Interesting must be relevant.** Use the deletion test: if cutting it loses no step of understanding, cut it or make it the example. In practice: humour is a character making the lesson's mistake; a story is the case being analysed; a fun fact is the evidence for the point. *Does not apply* to brand bumpers and credits, which sit outside the teaching window.

9. **A story as spine, sized to the concept.** A named character, a goal, a complication the concept resolves, a decision, a consequence. In practice: one scenario runs through the video and reappears in the checks. *Does not apply* well to expert audiences, who may learn more from direct exposition, and to reference content that people search for mid-task.

10. **Simple, warm, social visuals.** Pleasant palettes, friendly faces on key objects, and 2D characters who look at and gesture toward the content are small, consistent positives. In practice: flat characters over photoreal 3D; the character leaves the frame during dense explanation. *Does not apply* when the job requires recognising the real thing (then use realistic renders or screenshots).

11. **Voice: warm, conversational, well paced; modern TTS is fine.** Second-person scripts, short sentences, real pauses at boundaries, and a natural rate. In practice: aim for about 130 to 160 words per minute for new content (estimate; conventional practice range, not a tested optimum), slower on definitions and numbers. *Does not apply* to recap or review segments, which can run faster.

12. **Place music; do not pour it.** Music for openings, transitions, think-pauses and the close; ducked hard or absent under dense narration. In practice: a sting marks segment boundaries (which also aids event segmentation). *Does not apply* to second-language audiences or dense technical passages, where silence under the voice is safer.

13. **Segment by idea, keep the whole short, split rather than cut.** In practice: 3 to 8 minutes total, built from 60 to 120 second idea-segments (estimate) with a visible boundary and a checkpoint at each. If content needs more, make two videos. *Does not apply* to procedural walkthroughs that learners follow step by step while doing the task, where continuity matters more than brevity.

14. **Distrust your own polish.** Fluent, beautiful videos feel more instructive than they are. In practice: evaluate lessons with delayed check-question performance and error patterns, not completion, likes or "that was clear" ratings. Reserve the strongest visual treatment for one key idea per segment, so distinctiveness is preserved.

### 4.2 Where "catchy" and "learns well" pull together, and where they pull apart

**They pull together when the thing that is catchy is the thing to be learned:**

- A curiosity question whose answer is the lesson's key idea.
- A surprise that is a misconception breaking.
- A story whose plot turns on the concept.
- Motion that shows a real process changing.
- A drawing revealed in step with the explanation.
- A character who points to, looks at, and reacts to the content.
- Music that marks structure (segment boundaries, think-pauses).

**They pull apart when the catchy thing competes with the thing to be learned:**

- Fun facts, jokes and anecdotes not tied to the concept (seductive details).
- Fast cutting within an idea, which fragments events and raises load.
- Multiple simultaneous streams: captions plus labels plus effects plus music plus fast talk.
- A presenter or avatar on screen while a diagram needs full attention.
- Music under dense narration.
- High visual density per frame.
- Polished fluency that inflates confidence.

**How good designers resolve the tension (patterns the evidence supports):**

1. **Hook with the content, not around it.** The opening is the first question of the lesson.
2. **Separate "energy" moments from "load" moments in time.** Put music, character comedy and big transitions at boundaries and openings; keep explanations single-stream and calm. This follows the temporal pattern of where music costs (under narration) and where cues and presenters add load (during dense content).
3. **Use contrast, not constant intensity.** A quiet explanatory middle makes the reveal and the surprise distinctive.
4. **Let engagement devices double as learning devices.** A prediction freeze is both a cliffhanger and a generative activity; a character's mistake is both humour and refutation; a sting is both music and a segment marker.
5. **Match to expertise.** More story, cues and scaffolding for novices; less for experts.

### 4.3 How often should something change on screen, how long should segments be, and how fast should it go?

There is **no study that gives an optimal rate of visual change for instructional video.** Below, each number is marked as evidence or estimate.

**Segment length (per idea)**

- Evidence: the prequestion study used segments of about 2 minutes (Carpenter and Toftness 2017). Interpolated-testing studies inserted tests into 21 and 40 minute lectures at intervals of several minutes (Szpunar 2013; Jing 2016; my recollection is that the 21-minute lecture was split into four segments of roughly 5 minutes, not re-verified). Segmenting helps even when system-paced (Rey 2019).
- Estimate: **60 to 120 seconds of new content per segment** in a 3 to 8 minute video, ending in a boundary (hold, recap line, sting, or checkpoint). Shorter for dense or unfamiliar content, longer for narrative stretches.

**Whole video length**

- Evidence (observational engagement): MOOC engagement falls beyond about 6 minutes (Guo 2014); workplace course completion falls with length, sharply beyond 15 minutes (Easygenerator 2026, vendor); vendor video data shows drop-off beyond about 5 minutes (Wistia).
- Evidence (learning): no consistent evidence that shorter videos cause better learning for the same content.
- Estimate: **3 to 8 minutes is well supported for completion; aim near 4 to 6 for mandatory or low-interest topics**, and split rather than compress.

**Rate of visual change within a segment**

- Evidence: interaction peaks coincide with visual transitions (Kim 2014); higher visual density predicts pausing and dropout (2025 log study); long continuous animations lose information (transient information effect); memory is organised around event boundaries, and cuts help when they coincide with a real change (event segmentation research); in short-form social clips, many simultaneous stimuli were associated with shallower learning (Otto 2025).
- Estimate: **a meaningful visual change roughly every 5 to 15 seconds during explanation** (a new element drawn, a highlight moving to the next part, a state change), **with a 2 to 4 second hold after each key reveal** so it can be read and linked. A change should correspond to a change in meaning; a new scene should correspond to a new idea or step. Avoid cuts within a sentence unless they show the thing being said.
- Estimate: **no more than one attention cue active at a time**, and **no more than about 3 to 5 new visual elements per frame build** before a hold or a segment break (derived from the density and cueing evidence, not tested as a number).

**Narration pace**

- Evidence: comprehension of recorded lectures is surprisingly robust up to about 2x speed on immediate tests (Murphy 2022, 2024), but a 12-study meta-analysis finds acceleration raises load and lowers retention and transfer (Yang 2025). Visuals protect comprehension at speed (Murphy 2024).
- Estimate: **about 130 to 160 words per minute for new material, slower (and with pauses) for definitions, numbers and names; faster for recap.** Offer learner-controlled speed rather than baking in speed.

**Checkpoints and practice**

- Evidence: interpolated tests every several minutes in lectures (Szpunar 2013); 2-minute segments with prequestions (Carpenter 2017); spaced retrieval over days to weeks (Latimier 2021; Cepeda 2008; Kim 2019).
- Estimate: **one checkpoint per segment inside the video, 3 to 6 retrieval questions after, and 2 to 3 spaced follow-ups** at about 2 days, 1 to 2 weeks and 1 month for knowledge that must last months.

**Music**

- Evidence: cost mainly when overlapping narration (Moreno and Mayer 2000; Amato 2026, low weight); neutral to positive otherwise (Bai 2023).
- Estimate: music audible mainly in the first 5 to 10 seconds, at transitions, in think-pauses and at the close; ducked well below the voice (or absent) during explanation.

### 4.4 Open questions where nobody knows yet

1. **Do rhetorical "pause and think" prompts in a linear video work if learners do not actually respond?** The evidence base required responses. This is the single most important unknown for a non-interactive MP4 engine.
2. **What is the best visual change rate for learning (not for watch time)?** No study isolates it for instructional animation.
3. **Does animated emotional design help as much as static?** Wong and Adesope found motivational effects weaker for animated materials; the reason is unclear.
4. **Long-term and on-the-job effects.** Almost all findings use immediate or 1-week tests with students. Workplace behaviour change from animated video is barely studied.
5. **Does short-form video exposure cause attention decline, and does it change how people learn from 5-minute explainers?** Current evidence is correlational plus a few small experiments.
6. **Captions for native speakers with TTS narration.** Do captions help (accessibility, noisy offices, accent-free TTS) or hurt (redundancy) in animated diagrams? Evidence conflicts.
7. **AI presenters over time.** Do lower enjoyment and trust reduce voluntary return, completion or credibility for serious topics (safety, compliance), even when test scores match?
8. **Music with synthetic voices and second-language audiences.** Almost untested.
9. **Why effect sizes appear to shrink over time** in multimedia research (if they do): better controls, publication bias, or changing learners?
10. **How much surprise is optimal?** One 2022 study suggests memory is U-shaped with expectedness; how to calibrate this in scripts is unknown.
11. **How far characters and narrative help expert audiences** before expertise reversal makes them a cost.
12. **Whether distinctiveness effects hold in fully animated lessons** where everything moves, and how to preserve contrast.

## 6. Honest limits of this report

- Of the 118 numbered sources, 19 were read by me at abstract or page level (tagged [read]); the rest rely on search-result summaries or, in a few cases, recollection. Effect sizes quoted from summaries (marked [summary]) should be checked against the papers before being cited externally.
- Two music meta-analyses disagree in emphasis, and the more favourable one (Amato 2026) appears in a small, non-specialist venue.
- The playback-speed literature directly contradicts itself (Murphy versus Yang); I report both.
- Every numeric "range" in Section 4.3 not attributed to a study is an estimate for the team to test, ideally with A/B comparisons measured by delayed check-question performance rather than watch time.
- One source URL (de Jong 2010) was not surfaced by search and is given from recollection; treat as unverified.
